## 5. Extended paragraph (~700–900 chars)

V1) Down bottles are a small problem that behaves like a large one. A single fallen container ahead of the filler turns into a jam, then a spill, then an operator intervention and a clean-up — and on a high-speed line, fallen containers can stack on top of one another faster than anyone can react.

There are two answers. The mechanical down bottle reject guide relies solely on rail setup: using the geometry of the container against the position of the rails, it knocks fallen containers off the line while upright ones continue. There are no operating costs, no sensors and no compressed air, and it retrofits onto lines that already exist. Changeover between container sizes is simple.

Where the container or the application is trickier, a pneumatic system uses a vision trigger to identify and eject fallen containers — the same result, with electrical controls behind it.  [875 chars]

V2) The cheapest automation on a packaging line is usually the automation that removes a job nobody wanted. Watching for down bottles is that job.

The mechanical down bottle reject is a rail set, nothing more: the geometry of the container against the position of the rails sweeps fallen containers off the conveyor while upright containers continue downstream. It has no operating costs, retrofits to existing lines, and changes over simply between container sizes.

On lines where containers stack on each other when they fall, two reject units can be installed in sequence — the first clears the top container, the second clears what remains. On lines where the container defeats a mechanical approach altogether, a pneumatic reject triggered by a vision system does the same work under electrical control.  [806 chars]

**"What does it cost to run?"**
Nothing, in the mechanical version. There's no air and no sensors — the rails and the container geometry do the work.

No. It retrofits to the setup you already have. The mechanical system makes no changes to the conveyor design.
