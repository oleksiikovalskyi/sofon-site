## 8. Industry proof points

- **Beverage.** Frequent format changes on long lines make button-press changeover the difference between two SKUs a shift and four.
- **Food.** Effectiveness data from every changeover turns a soft target into a measured one, which is where most improvement programmes stall.
- **Pharmaceutical.** Quarantined and access-restricted areas can be adjusted without entry, and settings are recalled identically every time.
- **Personal care.** Wide portfolios mean constant resetting; recipe recall removes the operator variable across the whole range.
- **Household chemicals.** Washdown-capable automation survives the cleaning regime these lines run on.
- **Automotive and lubricants.** Large container ranges and long rail runs are exactly where manual adjustment consumes the most labour.
- **Contract packaging.** New customers arrive with new formats; adding a recipe is faster than training a technique.

## 9. Booth answers

**"We already have adjustable rails."**
Then you're most of the way there. Automation integrates easily with existing mechanical adjustable guide rails — it's an upgrade, not a replacement.

**"Do we have to automate the whole line?"**
No. The distributed architecture lets you automate one difficult section — overhead rails, quarantined areas — and extend later.

**"Will it work with our controls?"**
It integrates into an existing control system or runs stand-alone, with multiple controls platforms and motor options, plus an optional packaging machine language interface.

**"What data do we actually get?"**
Line and machine information for overall equipment effectiveness transparency and quality control, from every changeover rather than from a sample.

**"How long does a changeover take then?"**
Seconds, at the touch of a button, with no fine tuning afterwards.

**"What's the installation like?"**
Quick-disconnect cabling, and on larger systems combined control and power distribution nodes to keep cabling and installation cost down.
