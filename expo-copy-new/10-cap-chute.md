## 8. Industry proof points

- **Beverage.** High-speed capping starves quickly when the chute bridges; three-axis geometry set to the closure keeps flow stable at rate.
- **Food.** Wide closure ranges across SKUs are handled by adjustment rather than by a chute rail set per cap.
- **Pharmaceutical.** Small closures in regulated areas benefit from a washdown-capable chute with no springs or seals to service.
- **Personal care.** Pump, trigger and decorative closures are the hardest to convey; independent top-rail adjustment is often what makes them stable.
- **Household chemicals.** Trigger sprays and child-resistant closures each need their own chute geometry, recalled by number rather than rebuilt.
- **Automotive and lubricants.** Large, heavy closures load the rails; robust mounts and couplings hold the setting under that load.
- **Contract packaging.** A new customer's closure is a chute setting rather than a new chute — which is the difference between days and weeks.

## 9. Booth answers

**"Our caps jam in the chute."**
Almost always a geometry mismatch. Three-axis adjustment lets you set left, right and top rails to the actual closure instead of approximating it.

**"Our closures aren't round."**
Oblong, asymmetrical, tapered and odd-shaped closures are the design case, not the exception.

24 metres from one location, in under ten seconds, to plus or minus 1 mm.

**"How do operators know the setting is right?"**
Numerical displays. The setting is confirmed before the run, and there's no fine tuning afterwards.

No springs, no seals, no air pressure, no energy. It's washdown capable and effectively maintenance free.
